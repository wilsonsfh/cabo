Jack: triggering is optional; once triggered you must swap one own and one opponent card without looking, unless no legal pair exists.

Queen: choose one own and one opponent card, look privately at both, then explicitly swap or keep them. This is the implemented interpretation.

Matching cards & scoring

Replace several own cards only if their values match. A failed match exposes them and adds the draw; selecting three or four adds another penalty card. Public discard replacements stay face up. Deck replacements stay hidden.

Caller lowest or tied: 0 points; otherwise their sum +10. Everyone else scores their sum. No jokers, Kamikaze, or snap/slap. Quick is exactly four rounds. Classic ends above 100; exactly 100 resets to 50 once. Deck exhaustion also ends a round after the current action resolves.

Two-player local prototype. Online play uses this server, which knows the full deck. No official CABO affiliation. Rooms and chat are kept only in server memory; restarting clears them.

A memory.
A few rules.

Pip is a local, rule-based bot. It runs in your browser, with no language model or remote AI service.

It remembers what it sees.

Like you, Pip starts by peeking at two cards. Its decision code receives a filtered view: hidden cards have no rank or value. It remembers peeks and public reveals, tracks known swaps, and forgets cards replaced out of sight. It cannot inspect your hidden hand.

It makes a simple estimate.

Unknown cards count as 7 in Pip’s estimate. It draws from the deck, replaces matching groups it remembers, or trades its estimated highest card for a lower draw. Otherwise, it discards. It stacks when a remembered rank matches the discard.

It uses powers to learn and trade.

For a peek, Pip prefers a hidden card it does not remember. For a swap, it targets its estimated highest card and your estimated lowest. After a Queen’s private look, it swaps only if your card is lower.

It knows when to call.

Pip calls when its estimated hand is 9 or less, or the round reaches turn 22. Ordinary decisions pause for 2.2 seconds; private reveals pause for 3.2 seconds. These are fixed rules, so Pip can be predictable—and its estimates can be wrong.
